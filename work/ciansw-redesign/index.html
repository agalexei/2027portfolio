A clearer first step for insurance advice

I redesigned the CIANSW website around the decisions people arrive with: what cover to explore, who to speak to and how to get help with a claim.

The starting point

The original brokerage website established an online presence and brought services, products and claims information together. When I revisited it, the opportunity was in how people found their way through that information. On a small screen, an explanation of the brokerage can take up most of the first view. A business owner looking for cover and a client who needs to make a claim arrive with different questions and different levels of urgency.

I used the earlier and redesigned screen exports to audit the hierarchy, wording and next step on six routes. I treated the patterns in the screens as design evidence, with customer behaviour and conversion still to be measured.

Where I used the design trends

Answer-first pages
Each opening answers a visitor’s immediate question and offers an action. I kept these answers in ordinary headings and HTML text so they are available before interaction and can be read by search crawlers.
Scroll-led stories
On Home, I used finding cover, getting advice and making a claim as chapters of the service. A desktop side rail gives the story a sense of progress. Each chapter is also a normal anchor destination, and the layout stacks on mobile so people can read in order or jump to the part they need.
Task and product grids
I used grids to separate choices with comparable weight, then kept product detail available on demand. The home layout gives the main exploration path a little more room while retaining a clearly labelled claims route.
Frosted layers and soft depth
I limited glass effects to the sticky navigation and photo label. Both retain a solid or near-opaque background when blur is unavailable. I borrowed the softer shadows associated with neumorphism for grouping, with borders, text and focus outlines carrying the meaning of controls.
Sharper, lighter imagery
I kept photography where it establishes place or shows people at work, then used responsive WebP variants. The trade-off was to spend image weight on context and keep product, claims and enquiry pages led by text and controls.

01 · Home

Give each visitor a place to start

Compare mobile screens
Before
The earlier homepage opened with the brokerage name and a broad promise about protection, then led into individual insurance products. The copy did the work of introducing the firm, but the first decision still depended on knowing whether to browse, ask for advice or get claims help.
Decision
I kept Sydney visible as a point of familiarity and set national reach alongside it. The live headline leads with insurance advice for Australian businesses. I moved the 30+ years of general insurance experience into a supporting line so it could establish credibility without making the headline sound like a claim about the age of the business.
Why it matters
I gave prospective and existing clients different next steps. A quote is the main action, while claims support stays visible alongside it. The task cards then distinguish finding cover, getting help with a claim and talking to a broker. The Sydney Harbour image supports the local cue without taking the place of the message.

02 · Our products

Make a large catalogue easier to navigate

Compare mobile screens
Before
The earlier page opened with a general introduction and then presented a long sequence of individual insurance products.
Decision
I regrouped the catalogue around four starting points: business and liability, trades and construction, vehicles and marine, and personal cover. Each card introduces what the cover relates to. Where there is more detail, it sits behind a labelled disclosure. A product-specific quote link carries the selected cover into the enquiry.
Why it matters
The categories give visitors a way to narrow the list without knowing every policy name. Keeping detail optional makes the first pass shorter, while a visible broker-advice path gives unsure visitors a next step. The copy keeps product information separate from an offer or a decision about individual cover.

03 · Our services

Explain what the broker actually does

Compare mobile screens
Before
Longer sections described who the brokerage was, what it did, its experience and its support with claims. The page already had FAQs, but readers still had to assemble the service journey from several paragraphs.
Decision
I organised the core services around finding cover, reviewing existing cover and getting claims support. Each has a short explanation and a destination that follows from the task. I kept the FAQs and brought questions about geographic reach and not knowing which cover to choose into the published page.
Why it matters
The service page can now answer the question ‘What will a broker help me do?’ before a visitor reads the detail. Specific verbs such as review and lodge make the offer easier to understand than repeated claims about personalised service.

04 · Make a claim

Put the next action first under stress

Compare mobile screens
Before
The earlier design introduced the firm’s support, then explained what the team did and how to make a claim. The direct action sat further down the page.
Decision
I moved call and email actions into the opening section and followed them with a short preparation checklist for policy details, what happened and any evidence available. I also made room for a reassuring instruction to contact the team if some details are missing.
Why it matters
People can act before reading the full process. The checklist supports a more useful first conversation without making evidence feel like a prerequisite for getting help. The wording promises practical help with paperwork and lodging, with no promise about the result of the claim.

05 · Get a quote

Support people who know what they need and people who don’t

Compare mobile screens
Before
The earlier quote page led with a general message about tailored cover. The decision about what to tell the broker was less explicit in the screen.
Decision
I specified a searchable multi-select because an enquiry may involve several products. The redesign starts with Business, Personal or ‘I’m not sure’, then lets people choose cover optionally. Product links preselect a choice, and removable selections make the request easy to review before sending. The live implementation also reveals business fields when Business is selected.
Why it matters
The form can carry context forward for someone browsing a product while staying usable for someone who needs advice first. The lead and privacy hint explain why details are collected. The follow-up message describes a broker getting in touch, setting an expectation for a conversation rather than an instant price.

06 · Contact us

Let people choose how to start the conversation

Compare mobile screens
Before
The earlier page paired a general introduction with separate contact information further down.
Decision
I used a direct invitation to talk to a broker, placed phone and email in the opening section, and kept a distinct enquiry form. The supporting content names concrete reasons to contact the team, including cover questions, quotes and certificates of currency. The form marks optional fields and links to the privacy policy.
Why it matters
A visitor who wants to call can do so immediately, while someone who wants to explain the situation in writing has a clear place to do it. Contact stays a general help route and the quote form can ask the more specific questions a broker needs for that enquiry.

How I used AI and built the front end

I used AI as a design and implementation partner. I gave it the existing site, the audiences and the routes I wanted to clarify. I used it to compare patterns, turn ideas into working alternatives and iterate on copy in the layout. I chose which trends to apply, pushed for the searchable multi-select and revised the positioning until it kept both the Sydney identity and national reach. Those decisions gave the build a more specific brief than a request to make the site look modern.

The resulting front end uses static HTML, shared CSS and vanilla JavaScript. The quote picker uses labelled checkboxes inside a disclosure, filters the options, shows selected products as removable chips and uses query parameters to carry a product into the form. Escape returns focus to the picker summary. The home story rail uses IntersectionObserver to indicate the current section, with ordinary links as its baseline. Responsive grids, visible focus and reduced-motion handling support different devices and ways of navigating.

I also used AI-assisted iteration to shape concise search descriptions and direct answers in the page content. The release includes canonical URLs and structured business details. These give search systems useful context without making the interface depend on AI or claiming improved rankings.

CIANSW style guide

These tokens and patterns are drawn from the implemented redesign. The portfolio framing stays neutral so the CIANSW identity is visible in the screens and specimens.

Logo

Forest and deep green carry the brand, while paper and tint give long pages a quieter backdrop. Lime is reserved for the primary action and key emphasis. Links, field boundaries and focus rings give controls a cue beyond colour or shadow.

Type and spacing

Insurance adviceSystem sans · responsive display

Clear next steps, written for the person reading them.

Typeface
The redesigned site uses a native system stack with Segoe UI as a fallback. The earlier artwork specified Michroma and Libre Franklin. The system stack keeps the hierarchy readable without requiring a webfont download.
Scale
Body starts at 16 px with a 1.6 line height. Home display type scales from about 39 to 82 px and section headings from about 31 to 55 px at the default browser size. The page container is capped at 75 rem.
Shape and targets
Cards and imagery use a 1.25 rem corner radius. Primary buttons have a 46 px minimum height, while navigation and option controls generally use 44 px targets. A 3 px focus outline changes to lime on dark hero and footer surfaces.

Components and copy

Get a quoteMake a claim

Use the lime action for the primary task and a secondary style for a parallel route. Use cards for choices, disclosures for optional detail, and a form panel with visible labels for enquiries. Write labels as actions, use plain descriptions before policy detail, and say what happens after a form is sent. Keep disclosures and privacy information available without burying the customer’s next step.

What shipped and what I’d measure next

The redesign is live with distinct paths for cover, quotes, broker advice and claims. The published HTML and front-end code show the redesigned navigation, searchable quote selection and task-focused content. I’m treating those as delivery outcomes. I haven’t verified conversion uplift, search performance or end-to-end delivery to the brokerage inbox.

I’d validate whether people can find claims help from Home, whether product preselection carries through to completed enquiries, and which requests arrive with enough context for a useful broker follow-up. I’d pair those checks with a confirmed test of both form submissions and short task-based usability sessions.

For a client with a service that’s difficult to explain online, this is the work I can take on: identify the decisions your customers need to make, write and design a clearer way through them, and build a site your team can maintain.
